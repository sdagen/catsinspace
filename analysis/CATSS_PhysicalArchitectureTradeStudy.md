## Variant Rationales

### Monolithic Core Habitat

- Core concept: Single primary habitat bus integrates life support and caretaking systems with limited external growth ports.
- Expected strengths: Lowest integration overhead, compact crew and cat operations, straightforward safe-haven consolidation.
- Main penalties and risks: Poor growth efficiency, larger single-module launch pressure, concentrated single-point maintenance burden.
- Likely requirement pressure points: SR-038, SR-039, SR-040, SR-042
- Affordability evidence: SR-039 and SR-040 penalize the large integrated launch package, but shared systems lower near-term module count.
- Growth evidence: SR-038 is weak because expansion depends on attaching support elements to a tightly integrated bus.
- Resupply evidence: SR-006, SR-019, SR-028, and SR-042 are helped by centralized stores but mass concentration raises logistics sensitivity.
- Welfare evidence: SR-010, SR-011, SR-014, and SR-015 benefit from one large contiguous habitat with integrated refuge zoning.
- Safety evidence: SR-033, SR-035, and SR-037 score well because safe haven, emergency power, and suppression systems are concentrated.
- Autonomy evidence: SustainLifeSupport and OperateStation allocate cleanly to integrated automation and ECLSS bays supporting SR-030 and SR-031.

### Distributed Habitat Cluster

- Core concept: Several smaller habitat modules cluster around a shared utility node and dedicated service elements for growth-driven expansion.
- Expected strengths: Best incremental growth path, strong social separation options, natural compartmentalization for quarantine and damage isolation.
- Main penalties and risks: Highest interface and maintenance complexity, repeated shell overhead, more demanding inter-module logistics.
- Likely requirement pressure points: SR-019, SR-029, SR-030, SR-041, SR-042
- Affordability evidence: SR-039 and SR-040 suffer from repeated pressure shells, berthing hardware, and node-to-node utilities.
- Growth evidence: SR-038 and SR-041 are strongest because habitat capacity can be added as independent pods around the cluster.
- Resupply evidence: SR-019, SR-029, and SR-042 are harder because consumables and waste handling must be balanced across more modules.
- Welfare evidence: SR-010, SR-011, SR-014, and SR-015 benefit from natural separation of social groups into discrete habitats.
- Safety evidence: SR-033 and SR-035 improve through compartment isolation, though SR-037 must serve more distributed loads.
- Autonomy evidence: OperateStation and CareForColony require broader network coordination to meet SR-030 and SR-031.

### Service-Core With Replaceable Habitat Pods

- Core concept: A central infrastructure spine carries power, comms, life support, and docking while habitat pods are replaceable or incrementally added.
- Expected strengths: Best balance of reuse, scalable growth, and controlled interface standardization without full distributed overhead.
- Main penalties and risks: Service core remains a concentration point, pod interfaces must be tightly standardized, and habitat replacement logistics stay nontrivial.
- Likely requirement pressure points: SR-033, SR-038, SR-039, SR-041
- Affordability evidence: SR-039 and SR-040 improve because the service core is reused while smaller pods stay inside launch envelope targets.
- Growth evidence: SR-038 and SR-041 are strong because pods attach through a standardized service spine without redesigning the core.
- Resupply evidence: SR-006, SR-019, SR-028, and SR-042 benefit from centralized utilities and staged pod resupply via one berthing node.
- Welfare evidence: SR-010, SR-011, SR-014, and SR-015 are met with dedicated habitat pods, though contiguous free volume is lower than the monolith.
- Safety evidence: SR-033, SR-035, and SR-037 remain solid with a dedicated safe-haven module and isolation-capable pod interfaces.
- Autonomy evidence: Direct allocation of life support and operations into the core spine supports SR-030 and SR-031 with simpler coordination than the cluster.
